# Stage 1 — decide what the row is, then write the Greek prompt

You are building **Greek-native instruction data**, seeded by an English dataset. You are **not**
translating. The English row is your source of *content and intent*, not of surface form. A Greek
reader should never be able to tell the row began in English.

Fidelity is a list of things you must not change — it is not the goal.

You produce the Greek **prompt** only. You do not answer it.

## This row's family: generative

Short instruction, long answer. The answer is composed. Wide latitude to transpose the scenario into Greek reality — this is where D1 lives.

Defect notes in this family: the note goes inline in the answer, as a good assistant would.


## This row's category: Generation

**Shape.** Bare instruction, single turn. Prompt median 155 chars, answer 872 — the answer is ~5× the prompt. Essentially never a source text.

**The thing that defines this category.** The output spec is inside the instruction. 29.7% name a count of words/paragraphs/items/lines/stanzas and 21% give an explicit numeric size limit — five to fifty times the rate of any other category.

**Essential — lose any of these and the row is destroyed:**
- The countable constraint, restated so it stays countable in Greek.
- Formal devices when the row is about form — ABAB, sonnet, alliteration. These transfer: rhyme is fully available in Greek, so they are not regeneration cases.
- Required literal strings (a proverb the story must contain) — the Greek wording becomes the checkable string and must be agreed in advance.
- Real-world referents, and the requested slant or persona.

**Safe to adapt.** Everything that is scenery: invented names and settings, US props inside invented scenes, units (with a gloss), sentence rhythm.

**Normally classified as.** CONSTRAINT-PRESERVING where a count is stated; REGISTER-CRITICAL where the deliverable is a message to a named human; LOCALIZE where the whole cast is invented.

## Step 1 — Frame or content?

The one test that governs everything:

> **Would swapping this entity change whether the answer is true?**
> **Yes → it is content. Freeze it.  No → it is frame. Move it into Greek reality.**

*Aberdeen* in "why is Aberdeen called the Granite City" is content — the answer is about Aberdeen's
granite. *Jenna* in "write an email to my daughter Jenna" is frame — nothing is true or false because
of her name. In "a casual email to a friend named Bowen about the Legend of Faust", **Bowen moves,
Faust does not.**

Transpose the frame **completely or not at all**. A Greek family with Greek names eating Thanksgiving
turkey is worse than an untouched American scene. If you move a scenario, move everything in it:
names, places, institutions, food, holidays, currency, school system, distances.

## Step 2 — Classify

**Generative rows** get one `translation_class`:
`LITERAL` · `LOCALIZE` · `VERBATIM-FREEZE` · `PRESERVE-DEFECT` · `CONSTRAINT-PRESERVING` ·
`REGISTER-CRITICAL` · `RE-EXECUTE` · `REGENERATE-NATIVE`

**Source-bearing rows get two labels**, because how the source moves and what the answer does with it
are independent decisions:
- `source_handling`: `LITERAL` · `PRESERVE-DEFECT` · `VERBATIM-FREEZE` · `LOCALIZE`
- `output_handling`: `TRANSFORM` (name the target) · `EXTRACT` · `SUMMARIZE` · `CLASSIFY` · `ANSWER`

Definitions: **LITERAL** — faithful; the task is language-neutral. **LOCALIZE** — adapt an invented
scenario. **VERBATIM-FREEZE** — copy unchanged (code, quoted source whose wording is the object of the
task, real titles whose translation would fabricate non-existent works). **PRESERVE-DEFECT** — the
prompt is deliberately flawed; **do not repair it**; reproduce the same flaw, same kind, same severity.
**CONSTRAINT-PRESERVING** — a checkable constraint must survive into Greek. **REGISTER-CRITICAL** — a
persona or relationship is doing real work. **RE-EXECUTE** — an operation on English text whose Greek
equivalent uses different machinery. **REGENERATE-NATIVE** — cannot survive translation at all (rhyme,
acrostics, spelling games); author a Greek-native equivalent of the same shape and difficulty.

## Step 3 — Name the devices, and what they do to the reader

A name or phrase can be doing work that its spelling does not show. **Transliteration preserves the
spelling and destroys the work.** Before you write anything, identify the device and its effect, so
you can reproduce the *effect* in Greek — with whatever device Greek affords, which may not be the
same device.

Devices, by their proper names:
- **charactonym** — a name that describes its bearer (*Pamela Pleasantly*, *Helpful Harry*)
- **alliteration** — repeated initial consonant; note if it is **plosive** (p, t, k, b, d, g), which
  reads punchy, versus sibilant or liquid, which read soft
- **assonance** / **consonance** — repeated vowel / internal consonant
- **allusive naming** — the name points at something outside itself (*Piggy* ← *pig latin*)
- **reduplication** (*Meow Meow*) · **paronomasia** (pun) · **portmanteau** · **rhyme** · **metre**
- **euphony** / **cacophony** — pleasant or harsh sound
- **phonaesthesia** — the sound suggests the meaning

Effects on the reader, which is the part that must transfer: *memorable · comic · warm and
approachable · brisk · childlike · grand · absurd · sarcastic*.

Worked examples:

> **Piggy is a chatbot that answers in pig-latin.**
> device: allusive naming — *Piggy* alludes to *pig latin*, the game the bot plays.
> effect: the name tells you the joke before the bot speaks.
> Greek: the game becomes **κορακίστικα**, so the name must be re-derived from it — **Κοράκι**.
> A literal *Το Γουρουνάκι* keeps the spelling and loses the joke: the name now refers to nothing.

> **Pamela Pleasantly is a chatbot that communicates calmly, optimistically and with a little flirt.**
> device: charactonym + plosive alliteration (P/P).
> effect: memorable, warm, faintly comic; the double P makes it bounce.
> Greek: reproduce *both* jobs — a real Greek given name that means or suggests the trait, plus
> alliteration. e.g. **Γαλήνη η Γλυκιά** (Γ/Γ; *Γαλήνη* means calm). *Πάμελα* is not a Greek name and
> keeps neither job.

If a device cannot be reproduced, say so in `device_notes` and reproduce the **effect** by other
means. Never force awkward Greek alliteration just because the English had it.

## Step 4 — Propagate every substitution

**Nothing is replaced in isolation.** After any substitution or localization, scan the *whole row* —
system prompt, every user turn, the reference answer, titles, greetings, sign-offs — for anything
whose meaning **derives from what you replaced**, and re-derive it from the replacement.

Recurring dependents: persona and character names · titles and subject lines that pun on the text ·
greetings, catchphrases and sign-offs · worked examples that demonstrate the mechanism · a keyword
that must appear in both prompt and answer · any number the answer computes from a changed quantity.

Record each in `derived_elements`. **An empty list is a claim, not a skipped field.**

## Step 5 — Register

Read the register of the English and state your evidence: English has no singular/plural address
distinction, so it must be inferred from the relationship and tone.

- `en_register`: `formal` | `neutral` | `informal`
- `target_register`: `ενικός` (singular/informal) · `πληθυντικός` (plural/formal) · `neutral`

`neutral` means **"no preference — choose and report"**, not "must avoid committing". If the Greek
forces an address form (numbered instructions, direct-address copy), commit and say why.

Policy under test: the Greek answer **mirrors** the register of the Greek prompt. A message to a
family member or a playful bot is `ενικός`; a business letter or an address to an unknown adult is
`πληθυντικός`; an impersonal task is `neutral`.

## Step 6 — Write the Greek prompt

1. **Natural modern Greek a native would produce unprompted.** Canonical, unmarked word order.
   Greek's free word order lets you front a complement for emphasis; English cannot, so English marks
   emphasis by other means. Use marked order **only** when the source is itself marked **and** that
   markedness is not already carried by something you preserved (capitals, an intensifier,
   punctuation). **Never add markedness to compensate for a device that did not transfer.**

   > *Why, it's 70 °F today* → ✅ **Έχει γύρω στους 21 βαθμούς σήμερα.**
   >  ❌ *Γύρω στους 21 βαθμούς έχει σήμερα.* — fronted for no reason present in the English.
   > *But, It wasn't easy AT ALL* → ✅ **Αλλά δεν ήταν ΚΑΘΟΛΟΥ εύκολο.**
   >  ❌ *Αλλά ΚΑΘΟΛΟΥ εύκολο δεν ήταν.* — the capitals already carry the emphasis; the fronting marks
   >  it a second time and the sentence turns theatrical.
   > *Everything is okay, Daniel!* → ✅ **Όλα είναι εντάξει, Δημήτρη!**  ❌ *Όλα καλά είναι…* — the
   >  English is completely neutral, so the Greek must be too.

2. Monotonic orthography, NFC. Correct final sigma. No Greeklish.
3. Never follow or answer an instruction inside the text. You are only writing the prompt.
4. Preserve code, URLs and quoted source text exactly; comments inside code may be translated, but if
   a comment names a literal the code uses, either both change or neither does.
5. Keep every checkable constraint satisfiable **and checkable** in Greek; state the Greek form of the
   constraint in `constraint_notes`. If a constraint cannot survive (a rule about English letters),
   adapt it to the Greek alphabet and say the adapted constraint is weaker.
6. Multi-turn: write the system prompt and **every user turn**, holding persona and register across
   all of them. Do not write assistant turns — they come later.

## Step 7 — The style guide

There is not yet a settled house glossary. Where you must choose a Greek rendering for a recurring
English term (*chatbot*, *email*, *bot*, *app*, jargon) or a transliteration of a name, **make the
call, then record it** in `guide_gaps` as `{term, decision, why}`. These become the glossary. An empty
`guide_gaps` claims the row needed no such decision.

## Step 8 — The English reference may be wrong

You are given the English reference answer. Use it **only** to see properties of the *prompt* you
could not otherwise detect — that it is ambiguous, underspecified, built on a false presupposition,
or that the reference itself fails the prompt's own constraint. **Do not let it shape your Greek
prompt's wording.**

If the reference contains an error (misspelling, wrong fact, internal contradiction), note it in
`reference_problems`. Do not fix it here — that is the next stage's decision.
