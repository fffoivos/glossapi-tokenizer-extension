# Revisiting Few-sample BERT Fine-tuning (Zhang, Wu, Katiyar, Weinberger, Artzi; ICLR 2021; arXiv:2006.05987)

**Source:** arXiv:2006.05987  |  **Topic:** Embedding LR  |  `txt/llrd-bert-finetuning.txt`, `pdf/llrd-bert-finetuning.pdf`

**TL;DR.** An empirical study of BERT-Large few-sample fine-tuning instability that pins the main cause on the Adam debiasing omission, shows the top BERT layers are a poor init (Re-init helps), and argues longer training stabilizes; it surveys LLRD as one prior-work stabilizer whose benefit largely disappears once Adam debiasing is restored.

## Key load-bearing facts
- Scope/era caveat: this is a SUPERVISED few-sample classification fine-tuning study on BERT-Large (RTE/MRPC/STS-B/CoLA + 1k-downsampled GLUE), NOT a continued-pretraining or LLM paper. It does not propose LLRD; LLRD is cited as prior work (Howard & Ruder, 2018) and tested as one of several surveyed methods (Sec 7).
- LLRD direction (the load-bearing fact): 'LLRD ... applies higher learning rates for top layers and lower learning rates for bottom layers ... decrease the learning rate layer-by-layer from top to bottom. The goal is to modify the lower layers that encode more general information less than the top layers that are more specific to the pre-training task' (Sec 7.1, lines 1027-1033). So lower/bottom layers (nearer the input/embeddings) get a LOWER LR -- the project's claimed direction is correct.
- LLRD hyperparameter grid actually used (Appendix H, lines 2480-2481): initial LR searched in {2e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4}; layerwise decay rate in {0.9, 0.95}. These are the concrete recipe numbers from THIS paper -- decay 0.9-0.95 per layer, top-to-bottom.
- Critical caveat the doc likely omits: the paper's own experiments find LLRD's benefit is inconsistent and largely vanishes once Adam debiasing is restored. 'LLRD and Mixout show less consistent performance impact' and these stabilizers 'potentially ease the optimization difficulty brought by the debiasing omission ... when we add the debiasing, the positive effects are reduced' (Sec 7.2, lines 1067-1072). The paper is skeptical of LLRD, not an endorsement.
- The paper's actual layer-related contribution is Re-init, not LLRD: re-initializing the pooler + top L Transformer blocks (L in {1..6}) from N(0, 0.02^2) consistently improves mean performance and reduces variance; 'the top L layers of BERT are potentially overspecialized to the pre-training objective' (Sec 5, lines 690-721). This is a complementary insight: top layers are MORE pre-training-specific and benefit from larger change, which is consistent with LLRD giving them a higher LR.
- Headline baseline setup (Sec 3, lines 177-184): BERT-Large uncased, batch 32, dropout 0.1, peak LR 2e-5, 3 epochs default (argued sub-optimal), 10% linear warmup then linear decay, grad-norm clip 1.0, 20 random seeds. Main result: the Adam debiasing omission in 'BERTAdam' is the primary cause of degenerate fine-tuning runs.
- Generalization caveat: the LLRD finding is at supervised GLUE fine-tuning scale with 20-seed model selection on small datasets; the paper makes no claim about embedding LR or layerwise decay in continued-pretraining or 8B-scale LLMs.

## Citation audit — what CURRENT_HYPERPARAMETERS.md cites this for

**Claim:** Layerwise LR decay (LLRD): lower layers / embeddings get a lower LR -- supports that the documented direction is lower, not higher, for embeddings.
**Verdict:** `CONFIRMED`
**Evidence:** Sec 7.1 (lines 1027-1033): 'Layer-wise Learning Rate Decay (LLRD) ... applies higher learning rates for top layers and lower learning rates for bottom layers. This is accomplished by setting the learning rate of the top layer and using a multiplicative decay rate to decrease the learning rate layer-by-layer from top to bottom. The goal is to modify the lower layers that encode more general information less than the top layers that are more specific to the pre-training task.' Bottom layers (closest to input/embeddings) explicitly get the LOWER learning rate. Appendix H (lines 2480-2481) confirms a multiplicative decay rate of 0.9 or 0.95 applied top-to-bottom, which strictly lowers the LR as you descend toward the embeddings.

## How we use it
Cite this paper for the DIRECTION and a concrete decay range of LLRD: a per-layer multiplicative decay of 0.9-0.95 applied top-to-bottom, so layers nearer the input (and by extension the embedding/lower layers) receive the lowest learning rate and the top layers the highest (Sec 7.1, Appendix H). This directly supports CURRENT_HYPERPARAMETERS.md's claimed direction -- lower, not higher, LR for embeddings/lower layers. Do NOT cite it as proof that LLRD reliably improves training: the paper is a skeptic (it found LLRD's gains inconsistent and mostly attributable to compensating for the Adam debiasing bug; lines 1067-1072). For a Greek CPT run on Apertus-8B note the scope gap: this is supervised GLUE fine-tuning of BERT-Large with 20-seed selection on tiny datasets, not continued pretraining at 8B scale, so the 0.9-0.95 decay and 2e-5/5e-5/1e-4 LR grid are starting heuristics, not validated CPT numbers. Independently worth flagging for the embedding-extension/CPT design: this paper's stronger, better-supported finding is that BERT's top layers are over-specialized to the pretraining objective (Re-init, Sec 5) -- the inverse intuition (lower layers carry general info, change them less) is exactly what motivates assigning new/extended embeddings and adjacent layers their own LR schedule during vocabulary-extension CPT.
