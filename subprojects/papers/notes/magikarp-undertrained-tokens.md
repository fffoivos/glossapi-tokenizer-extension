# Fishing for Magikarp: Automatically Detecting Under-trained Tokens in Large Language Models (Bartolo & Land, Cohere; arXiv:2405.05417)

**Source:** arXiv:2405.05417  |  **Topic:** Vocabulary cutoff  |  `txt/magikarp-undertrained-tokens.txt`, `pdf/magikarp-undertrained-tokens.pdf`

**TL;DR.** Embedding-weight indicators (output-embedding cosine distance to a mean unused-token reference, or input-embedding norm for untied models) plus repetitive-prompt verification reliably detect "glitch"/under-trained tokens, which make up roughly 0.1-1% of vocabularies and arise mainly when a tokenizer is trained on data unlike the model's training data.

## Key load-bearing facts
- Two model-weight indicators. (1) For models with TIED embeddings: cosine distance C(Eout, uref) between each output-embedding row and the mean embedding uref of a manually chosen set of known-unused/under-trained reference token IDs (e.g. <unused_token123>, or indices above the tokenizer vocab size). Under-trained tokens cluster in a shared output-embedding direction ('moving away from the mean output vector'). (2) For models WITHOUT tied embeddings (the majority): the L2 norm of the input embedding L2(Ein) -- under weight decay, input rows of tokens never seen in inputs decay toward zero; this needs no reference-token set. Section 2.2, lines 144-210.
- Verification by prompting, not by a frequency count. Candidates are ranked by the indicator; the top 2% (excluding partial-UTF-8 and unreachable tokens) are verified with three repetitive prompts at temperature 0, generating 3 tokens and taking the max target-token probability across prompts. A token is 'confirmed' under-trained if that maximal output probability is < 1% (p<0.01). Section 2.3 lines 211-221; Appendix B / Table 3, lines 1208-1315.
- Prevalence: 'typically around 0.1-1% of the vocabulary' is severely under-trained; among the top-2% candidates tested, 5-50% pass the <1% verification threshold. Conclusion lines 819-822; Results lines 250-253.
- Indicators correlate with training-data token frequency across ~10 orders of magnitude on OLMo v1.7 (the one open model with public per-token counts) -- Figure 2, lines 584-587. Quantitative validation: applying verification to ALL tokens, 191/49,575 pass; testing only the top-2% catches 175 of those 191 (Figure 3, lines 232-236). So the indicator is predictive of training frequency, but the paper sets NO numeric occurrence threshold separating trained from under-trained.
- Root cause and recipe relevance for CPT: the dominant risk factor (besides large vocab) is a tokenizer trained on data DISSIMILAR to model training data -- 'Models which re-use a large external tokenizer, and then train on distinct data from scratch, are among those with the highest number of under-trained tokens' (lines 826-829). Byte-level BPE adds risk via under-trained intermediate fragments and partial-UTF-8 sequences (lines 290-294, 865-868). Single-document/single-repo merges can mint a token by themselves (StarCoder2 base64/Lorem-ipsum cases, lines 730-733, 861-864).
- Caveats the practitioner must carry: (a) output-embedding indicators are HEURISTIC, based on a working hypothesis of training dynamics, with a noisy indicator->behaviour relationship (Limitations, lines 891-905); (b) the input-norm indicator depends on the specific weight-decay and init scheme, which is often undocumented (lines 896-902); (c) tied-embedding models need a manually specified reference-token set, so the method is not fully automated for them (lines 887-890); (d) scope is BPE only -- Unigram models may behave differently (lines 924-928).
- Recommendations for anyone training/extending a tokenizer: keep pre-processing identical across tokenizer-training, model-training, and inference (especially carriage returns and special tokens appearing as plaintext); ensure tokenizer-training data is representative of model-training data; after building a tokenizer, encode+decode the whole vocab to catch UNREACHABLE tokens and check unused byte-fallback tokens; after a small training run, scan for under-trained tokens as a sanity check (lines 842-858).

## Citation audit — what CURRENT_HYPERPARAMETERS.md cites this for

**Claim:** The paper establishes an under-trained-token 'signature' usable as the basis for a noise-token cleanup of an extended tokenizer.
**Verdict:** `CONFIRMED`
**Evidence:** Section 2.2 defines the signature explicitly: under-trained output embeddings 'share a similar direction in output embedding space' and are detected via cosine distance to the mean unused-token vector C(Eout, uref) (lines 157-189); for untied models the input-embedding norm L2(Ein) is the indicator because un-input weight-decayed rows 'tend to zero' (lines 195-209). Section 2.3 confirms candidates via repetitive prompts checking for <1% output probability (lines 211-221). Prevalence ~0.1-1% of vocab (lines 819-822).

**Claim:** Kept Greek tokens 'fire >1,000x', so the under-training rationale for cutting them does not apply (CURRENT_HYPERPARAMETERS.md line 58).
**Verdict:** `OVERSTATED`
**Evidence:** The paper sets NO training-occurrence threshold (e.g. 1,000) that demarcates trained vs under-trained. Its operational criteria are an EMBEDDING-INDICATOR percentile (top 2%, dotted line in Fig. 3) and a VERIFICATION probability (<1% max output probability), not a firing count. Figure 2 only shows a continuous correlation between indicators and 'the number of times each token appears in the first epoch of training... across ten orders of magnitude' (lines 584-587) on a single model (OLMo v1.7), and notes such training counts are 'rarely publicly available' (lines 225-228). The under-trained tokens it confirms are ones 'rarely or never seen in training' / with 'no model training data occurrences' (lines 68-75, 287).
**Correction:** Reframe the cited support. Magikarp supports the QUALITATIVE direction ('tokens seen plentifully in training are not under-trained; under-trained tokens are the rarely/never-seen ones'), but it does NOT supply or endorse the specific '>1,000x' numeric cutoff -- that number is the project's own corpus-frequency heuristic. State it as: 'Under Magikarp's signature, tokens that recur abundantly in our CPT corpus fall in the well-trained tail, so the under-training rationale for cutting does not apply; the >1,000x figure is our own corpus-occurrence threshold, not a value the paper specifies.' For paper-faithful verification, the project should additionally run Magikarp's actual indicator/verification (post-CPT embedding cosine/L2 + <1% prompt check) rather than relying on corpus firing count alone.

**Claim:** Magikarp justifies the separate noise-token cleanup (distinct from the vocab-size cutoff).
**Verdict:** `PARTIAL`
**Evidence:** The paper does motivate removing problematic tokens (they 'occupy capacity... that could be better utilized', 'cause unwanted model outputs', and can 'circumvent guardrails', lines 77-83) and identifies the specific families to target: partial-UTF-8 fragments, unreachable tokens, unused byte-fallbacks, single-document/single-repo merge artifacts (lines 290-294, 730-733, 831-833). But the paper's method DETECTS under-trained tokens in an already-trained model; it does not prescribe a pre-training vocab-pruning ('cleanup') procedure, and explicitly notes some flagged tokens are deliberately kept for fine-tuning headroom (line 303).
**Correction:** Keep the citation for the noise-token TAXONOMY and detection signature (UTF-8 fragments, unreachable/byte-fallback, single-doc merges). Do not imply the paper prescribes pruning an extended tokenizer before CPT -- it diagnoses post-hoc. The cleanup design is the project's; Magikarp supplies the 'what to look for' and the verification test, not a removal recipe.
